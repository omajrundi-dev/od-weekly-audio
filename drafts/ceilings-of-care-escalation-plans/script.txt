Kate: The old problem was the standalone D N A C P R form, read as "not for much". Cambridge built the Universal Form of Treatment Options, which puts the resuscitation decision inside an overall plan. A before-and-after study in PLoS One in twenty thirteen found fewer harms in patients not for C P R, about thirteen fewer per thousand patient-days. Harms contributing to death fell from twenty-three of seventy-one to four of forty-four.
Tom: Before-and-after, one hospital.
Kate: Yes, hypothesis-generating. But the bias is real. A vignette study in Age and Ageing last year randomised geriatricians to the same deteriorating patient, with or without a D N A C P R. With it, about two-thirds would consider N I V, against over four in five without. I C U was considered by about one in ten, against one in four.
Tom: And ReSPECT?
Kate: The N I H R mixed-methods evaluation, twenty twenty-two. By late twenty nineteen about a fifth of acute hospitals used it. Patient or family involvement was recorded on about three-quarters of forms. Recorded harms were similar with or without a form. And the conversations they observed ranged from genuinely open, to persuasive, to simply announcing a decision already made.
Tom: That last category is where the law bites.
Kate: Locally, a Leeds analysis of twenty-three thousand ReSPECT plans found D N A C P R less often recorded for Asian and Black than White patients.
Tom: Right. The law. Start with Burke.
Kate: Twenty oh five. Paragraph fifty: the doctor decides which options are clinically indicated, the patient chooses among them. No legal obligation to provide something not offered, though the doctor should offer a second opinion.
Tom: And paragraph fifty-five.
Kate: "A patient cannot demand that a doctor administer a treatment which the doctor considers is adverse to the patient's clinical needs." But note paragraph fifty-three. Where a competent patient wants life-prolonging nutrition and hydration, withdrawing it to end his life would be murder. Burke isn't a licence.
Tom: Aintree v James, Supreme Court, twenty thirteen. Lady Hale.
Kate: Paragraph eighteen: the court has no greater powers than the patient; it can't order doctors to treat. Paragraph twenty-two: the question is whether giving the treatment is in his best interests, not whether withholding is. Paragraph thirty-nine: welfare in the widest sense, and consult the family about what he'd want. And paragraph forty-five: not the reasonable patient, this patient's point of view, though his wishes needn't prevail.
Tom: Then N versus A C C G in twenty seventeen. The court chooses only between available options.
Kate: Now D N A C P R. Tracey, Court of Appeal, twenty fourteen, a neuro-critical care patient. Article eight is engaged. Paragraph fifty-three: a presumption in favour of involving the patient, needing convincing reasons not to. Paragraph fifty-five: thinking C P R futile doesn't remove the duty to tell her.
Tom: And distress?
Kate: Not enough. It must be likely to cause harm. Longmore L J, paragraph eighty-nine, said the common law duty is the same.
Tom: And Winspear?
Kate: High Court, twenty fifteen, Blake J. Patient lacked capacity. D N A C P R put on overnight without phoning his mother, his lifelong carer. Breach of section four seven of the Mental Capacity Act, and of article eight. Paragraph fifty-two: she has no veto, but she's entitled to be consulted. And at paragraph fifty, a three a m call being inconvenient isn't the same as impracticable.
Tom: Now Townsend.
Kate: A man in I C U after multiple strokes, on dialysis. The team decided not to replace his line. The family applied. In February Theis J refused permission: no option for the court, because a capacitous patient couldn't compel dialysis either.
Tom: Classic Burke reasoning.
Kate: The Court of Appeal allowed the appeal, reasons on the third of March. Baker L J, paragraph sixty-nine: every decision about an incapacitated adult, including withdrawing life-sustaining treatment, is a best interests decision. "There is no carve out for clinical decisions."
Tom: So the court can now order dialysis?
Kate: No. The judgment restates the old rule: the court can never compel doctors to give treatment they consider clinically inappropriate. "But the decision is for the Court, not the clinicians." Paragraph seventy-two: where there's unresolved disagreement, the commissioning body must apply, and the hospital can't pre-empt that by unilaterally withholding or withdrawing treatment on clinical grounds.
Tom: That's the sentence that worries intensivists.
Kate: And it's changing behaviour. In Royal Free v R H in April, a young man in I C U with multi-organ failure, the doctors took legal advice in light of Townsend and started R R T, pressors and ventilation while the dispute went to court. Peel J then approved ceilings: R R T continued as palliation, ventilation and pressors only briefly to cover a line change, and no C P R. And he stressed the order was permissive.

Kate: Separate the two questions out loud. First: what can medicine realistically achieve? That's ours. Second: what would he want, given that? That's theirs to inform. Never ask them to decide.
